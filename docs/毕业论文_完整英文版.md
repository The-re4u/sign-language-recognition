## ABSTRACT

Sign language is the primary communication modality for over 70 million deaf individuals worldwide. For native sign language users, typing on a keyboard requires a cognitively demanding conversion from sign language concepts to Chinese grammatical structures and then to character-level encoding — a process that imposes high cognitive load and low expression efficiency. For congenitally deaf and elderly deaf populations whose sole proficient language is sign language, text-based input is functionally equivalent to communicating in a foreign language. It is therefore necessary to develop an approach that enables deaf individuals to communicate directly through their native gestural modality.

This thesis presents a lightweight, multimodal real-time sign language recognition system based on the MediaPipe visual pipeline. The system introduces a novel dual-hand combinatorial encoding protocol as its core interaction paradigm: the non-dominant hand selects one of seven semantic layers through mode gestures, while the dominant hand specifies meaning through one of ten content gestures, producing 70 unambiguous semantic slots from only 15 base gestures. The total trainable deep learning parameters are within 3.4 million, enabling CPU-only real-time inference.

To validate the proposed system, we conducted extensive experiments on a self-collected dataset of 1,311 samples with systematic domain randomization covering lighting, angle, and distance variations. The multimodal deep learning model achieved 82.8% test accuracy, representing a 51.7 percentage point improvement over the rule-based geometric classifier on the same real-user data, with 5-fold cross-validation confirming consistent performance (84.1% plus or minus 1.5%). Experimental results demonstrate that learning user-specific features from data effectively bridges the generalization gap that limits population-level anatomical thresholds in real-world scenarios.

Based on the proposed method, a complete sign language recognition prototype system was developed. Its features include real-time sign language translation, AI-assisted triage consultation with multi-turn symptom inquiry and department recommendation, one-click switching between the explainable rule engine and the deep learning model, and a scene-transferable architecture where semantic mappings, chain rules, and language model prompts are defined through JSON configuration files — enabling migration to banking, airport, smart home, and government service scenarios without code modification. This work demonstrates that treating sign language gestures as linguistic tokens in a pipeline culminating in large language model-based semantic reassembly can bridge the gap between isolated gesture classification and natural sentence output, providing a feasible solution for accessible communication in healthcare and public service settings.

# CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Background

Sign language is the primary communication modality for over 70 million deaf individuals worldwide, according to the World Federation of the Deaf. In China alone, approximately 20 million people experience hearing impairment, with Chinese Sign Language (CSL) serving as their native linguistic system. However, a profound communication barrier persists in daily life: the vast majority of hearing individuals do not understand sign language, and existing assistive technologies predominantly rely on text-based input methods that are fundamentally misaligned with the cognitive and linguistic patterns of deaf users.

For native sign language users, typing on a keyboard requires a cognitively demanding double conversion process: first, translating from sign language concepts to Chinese grammatical structures (two languages with fundamentally different word orders and grammatical features), and then encoding the Chinese grammar into character-level input through Pinyin or stroke-based input methods. This three-stage cognitive pipeline imposes significant mental load and reduces communication efficiency to a fraction of natural sign language expression speed. This approach both violates their linguistic autonomy and introduces systematic expressive distortion, as the grammatical structure of sign language (which is spatial, simultaneous, and highly iconic) does not map cleanly onto the linear, sequential structure of written Chinese.

The technological landscape has shifted dramatically in recent years. Markerless human pose estimation frameworks, particularly Google MediaPipe, have democratized access to real-time hand tracking. MediaPipe Hands detects 21 three-dimensional keypoints from a single RGB image at over 30 frames per second on commodity hardware, without requiring depth sensors, special gloves, or calibrated multi-camera setups. This breakthrough has opened the possibility of practical sign language recognition systems that can operate on ordinary laptop webcams — a critical requirement for real-world accessibility applications where specialized hardware is economically infeasible.

Wagh et al. [1] provided the first proof-of-principle clinical validation that single-camera MediaPipe tracking can extract clinically meaningful kinematic outcomes — including palm speed and Bivariate Variable Error (BVE) — from video data in stroke populations. [4] verified criterion-related validity against gold-standard marker-based systems, reporting correlation coefficients of r = 0.72-0.82 in patient populations with mean absolute deviation of 2.46 degrees, reducible to 0.81 degrees with Kalman filtering.

Despite these advances in perception technology, most existing sign language recognition (SLR) systems share a critical limitation: they focus on isolated word classification. The research community has largely treated SLR as a computer vision problem — given an image or video clip, classify which word is being signed. Furthermore, the integration of large language models (LLMs) for semantic interpretation of gesture sequences remains a largely unexplored direction, despite the rapid advancement of LLM capabilities in recent years with models like DeepSeek-V2 [25] demonstrating strong performance in Chinese-language medical dialogue tasks.

This thesis addresses both gaps simultaneously: it proposes a complete pipeline that treats gestures as linguistic tokens rather than mere visual patterns, and it integrates an LLM layer that converts raw gesture sequences into natural, contextually appropriate Chinese sentences. The system is validated in a hospital triage scenario — a real-world communication context where accurate, efficient information transfer between a deaf patient and medical staff is critical.

## 2.1 MediaPipe Hand Tracking and Clinical Validation

Google MediaPipe Hands represents a significant advance in markerless hand pose estimation, building on decades of progress in computer vision for human pose tracking. The framework employs a two-stage machine learning pipeline: a palm detection model identifies the approximate hand region in the image, followed by a hand landmark model that regresses 21 three-dimensional keypoint coordinates within the detected region. This anatomical coverage enables comprehensive hand pose reconstruction from a single RGB image without depth sensors.

The framework operates in real time on commodity hardware, achieving detection speeds exceeding 25 frames per second on CPU through the XNNPACK delegate. This performance profile makes it uniquely suitable for practical sign language recognition applications, where dedicated GPU hardware may not be available and low latency is critical for natural interaction. The system supports both IMAGE mode (single-frame detection, suitable for static images) and VIDEO mode (temporal tracking with hand presence continuity, suitable for continuous video streams).

Wagh et al. [1] provided the foundational methodological reference for this work. Specifically, the 17-dimensional kinematic feature set (including palm speed, fingertip velocity, inter-finger coordination, and trajectory stability) was designed following the measurement framework validated in Wagh et al.'s work.

## 2.2 Graph Convolutional Networks for Skeleton-Based Recognition

Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) extend the convolution operation from regular grid structures (such as image pixels) to irregular graph structures (such as skeletal joints connected by bones). This generalization is particularly well-suited to hand skeleton data, where the 21 keypoints naturally form a graph with bone connections as edges and joint coordinates as node features.

The foundational work in this area is the Spatial Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (ST-GCN) proposed by Yan et al. [6] at AAAI 2018. ST-GCN models the human skeleton as a spatio-temporal graph G = (V, E) where nodes V represent body joints at each time step and edges E represent both spatial connections (bones connecting joints within a frame) and temporal connections (the same joint across consecutive frames). The spatial graph convolution operation at layer l for node i aggregates features from its neighboring nodes j in the set N(i):

h_i^(l+1) = sigma( sum_{j in N(i)} W^(l) * h_j^(l) / c_ij )

where each node aggregates features from its skeletal neighbors through learnable weights. This message-passing mechanism, combined with temporal 1D convolutions along the time axis, enables joint modeling of spatial hand topology and motion dynamics. For a detailed mathematical treatment, the reader is referred to Yan et al. [6].

Since ST-GCN, the research community has developed numerous enhancements specifically for sign language recognition. Zhou et al. [7] at LREC-COLING 2024 proposed a multimodal spatio-temporal GCN model with gating mechanisms for isolated sign recognition, incorporating both skeleton data and handshape features through per-channel multi-layer convolution gating. The model achieved 80.8% Top-1 and 95.2% Top-5 accuracy on combined ASLLVD and WLASL datasets, demonstrating the value of multimodal skeleton-based approaches.

Liu et al. [8] introduced Asymmetric Multi-branch GCN (AM-GCN) for skeleton-based SLR, achieving 96.27% on AUTSL and 98.20% on CSL datasets. Their key innovation was an Asymmetric Multi-branch Temporal Convolution (MTC) module that uses dilated convolutions in asymmetric branches to expand the receptive field — a direct TCN enhancement built on top of GCN spatial features. The architecture also introduced Spatial-Temporal Fusion Attention (STFA) and Asymmetric Convolution Channel Attention (ACCA) mechanisms.

The hand-aware GCN (HA-GCN) [9] explicitly models hand skeletal topology through two adaptive hand sub-graphs: a structured hand graph (SH) based on anatomical bone connections and a parameterized hand graph (PH) learned from data. An adaptive DropGraph mechanism removes spatial and temporal redundancy, addressing overfitting on limited data — a technique directly relevant to the data-constrained setting of this thesis (1,311 samples). HA-GCN achieved 96.82% on AUTSL and 99.63% on the INCLUDE dataset.

Huang et al. [10] proposed synchronized and fine-grained head mechanisms for skeleton-based ambiguous action recognition, addressing cases where similar skeleton configurations map to different semantic actions — a problem directly analogous to the Two vs. Victory or Four vs. Nine disambiguation challenges in this system.

## 2.3 Temporal Convolutional Networks and SlowFast Architecture

Temporal modeling of gesture sequences requires architectures that can capture both fine-grained frame-to-frame dynamics and long-range temporal context spanning complete gesture phrases. Two families of approaches dominate the literature: recurrent neural networks (RNNs, including LSTM and GRU variants) and temporal convolutional networks (TCNs).

Temporal Convolutional Networks, introduced by Lea et al. [12] at CVPR 2017 for action segmentation and detection, use causal convolutions with dilated kernels to capture long-range temporal dependencies. Unlike recurrent networks, TCNs offer several advantages: (1) parallel computation across time steps (convolutions can be computed simultaneously rather than sequentially), (2) no vanishing/exploding gradient problems through the temporal dimension (gradients flow through fixed-depth convolutional paths rather than recurrent loops), (3) precise control over receptive field size through kernel size and dilation rate selection, and (4) easier export to inference formats (ONNX) since the computation graph is purely feed-forward.

The Slow pathway uses kernel size 7 with dilations [1, 2, 4], yielding a receptive field of 43 frames (approximately 1.7 seconds at 25 FPS) — sufficient to cover a complete gesture phrase including hold, transition, and stabilization periods. The general formula and theoretical analysis of dilated TCN receptive fields is provided by Lea et al. [12].

The GCAR model [13] demonstrated the parameter efficiency of TCN-based approaches for sign language. Their two-stream multistage graph convolution with attention and residual connection used only 0.69M parameters while achieving 90.31% accuracy on WLASL, 94.10% on PSL, and 99.75% on MSL. This result — competitive accuracy with less than 1M parameters — validates the TCN paradigm for resource-constrained sign language recognition.

The SlowFast architecture, originally proposed by Feichtenhofer et al. [11] at ICCV 2019 for video action recognition, introduced a key insight: visual semantics in video change slowly (a hand pose persists over many frames) while fine-grained motion occurs quickly (finger transitions happen between consecutive frames). Rather than processing all frames at a single temporal resolution, SlowFast employs dual pathways operating at different frame rates: a Slow pathway with a low frame rate and large convolutional kernels for capturing spatial semantics, and a Fast pathway with a high frame rate and small kernels for retaining fine temporal details. Lateral connections transfer information from the Fast pathway to the Slow pathway, enabling cross-scale feature fusion.

This dual-temporal-resolution design was adapted in this thesis as a SlowFast TCN for gesture sequence modeling. The key adaptations from video action recognition to gesture recognition are: (1) replacing 3D spatial-temporal convolutions with 1D temporal convolutions (since spatial features are already extracted by the GCN upstream), (2) using causal (rather than non-causal) convolutions to prevent future-frame leakage in real-time inference, and (3) reducing channel dimensions to maintain the sub-1M parameter budget for the temporal module. SF-TPGR [15] applied SlowFast to traffic police gesture recognition using skeleton heatmaps as input, achieving 79ms inference with 98.1% accuracy — demonstrating that SlowFast generalizes effectively beyond its original video action recognition domain.

The Faster-Slow network [16] introduced Behavioural Feature Enhancement (BFE) modules containing Motion Modules with motion-aware attention and Spatio-Temporal Channel enhancement for rapidly changing actions. This work is relevant to the motion encoder design in this system (Section 3.3.3), which similarly captures inter-frame dynamics through keypoint velocity fields.

## 2.4 Multimodal Fusion and Transformer Attention

The fusion of multiple modality streams — spatial geometry from keypoints, temporal dynamics from motion, and appearance features from RGB images — requires an architecture that can learn which modality to trust under different conditions. Early fusion approaches (simple concatenation) treat all modalities equally, failing to adapt when one modality is degraded (e.g., visual features are unreliable under low light, while keypoint geometry remains stable). Late fusion approaches (separate classifiers with voting) miss cross-modal interactions entirely. Attention-based fusion occupies a middle ground, enabling the model to dynamically weight modalities based on input quality.

Transformer architectures, introduced by Vaswani et al. [21] in "Attention is All You Need" at NeurIPS 2017, have become the dominant paradigm for multimodal fusion due to their ability to model pairwise interactions between all input tokens through multi-head self-attention. The multi-head self-attention mechanism, defined by Vaswani et al. [21], enables each modality token to attend to information from all other modalities through learned query-key-value projections. With h = 8 attention heads operating in parallel, the model can simultaneously capture different types of cross-modal relationships — for example, one head attending to visual features when spatial geometry is ambiguous, while another favors motion cues when the hand is static.

MSGCN [22] applied multimodal fusion to sign language recognition by combining skeleton-based GCN features with RGB-based deformable 3D convolutional features through a gating mechanism. Published in Sensors (MDPI) in 2025, their dual-modality framework used a decoupling GCN with self-emphasizing TCN for the skeleton stream and deformable 3D ResNet for the RGB stream, fused through learnable gates that control the contribution of each modality. Recent work [23] on multi-modal fusion with Transformer-enhanced learning demonstrated real-time continuous sign language recognition with low-latency inference specifically optimized for edge devices. Cross-attentive multi-cue fusion [24] extended this direction by explicitly modeling which visual cues (hand shape, orientation, position) contribute most to each recognition decision.

In this thesis, the CrossModalFusion module (Section 3.3.4) adapts the Transformer fusion paradigm with a key innovation: learnable modality type embeddings. Beyond the standard position embeddings used in Transformer architectures, each modality token is augmented with a learned embedding that encodes its source (visual, spatial, or motion). This enables the self-attention mechanism to distinguish not just which token to attend to, but which modality that token represents — a critical capability when the same 256-dimensional feature space is shared by fundamentally different information types.

## 2.6 Sign Language Recognition: From Classification to Semantic Understanding

The evolution of sign language recognition research reflects a broader trajectory in artificial intelligence: from hand-crafted features to deep learning, from isolated classification to sequential modeling, and — most recently — from pattern recognition to semantic understanding. Bragg et al. [28] provided a landmark interdisciplinary perspective on SLR, generation, and translation, bringing together researchers from computer vision, natural language processing, linguistics, and the Deaf community to identify research priorities. Their key findings remain relevant: (1) the Deaf community prioritizes communicative function over academic benchmark performance; (2) isolated word recognition, while technically impressive, does not address real communication needs; (3) integration of linguistic knowledge (grammar, semantics, discourse) is essential for practical systems.

Li et al. [29] introduced WLASL (Word-Level American Sign Language), a dataset of over 2,000 ASL word classes with 21,083 video samples, which has become the standard benchmark for isolated SLR. Rastgoo et al. [30] provided a comprehensive survey of deep learning approaches for sign language recognition, covering hand detection, feature extraction, sequence modeling, and multimodal fusion. Their analysis identified the trend toward skeleton-based methods (which are robust to background variation) and the need for continuous recognition (which bridges the gap to practical communication).

The critical gap that this thesis addresses is the integration of LLMs for semantic interpretation. Most existing SLR systems stop at classification — they output a word label and consider the task complete. But communication does not end at word recognition. Words must be assembled into sentences with proper grammar, adapted to conversational context, and expressed with appropriate tone and register. DeepSeek-V2 [25] and medical LLM applications [26][27] have demonstrated that LLMs can perform these functions for text-based medical dialogue. Extending this capability to gesture-based input — where the LLM receives not well-formed text but a sequence of semantic tokens with potential gaps, repetitions, and errors — represents a novel integration that this thesis explores.

## 2.7 Research Trend Analysis and Positioning

The literature reviewed in this chapter reveals a clear evolutionary trajectory in sign language recognition research over the past decade. This trajectory can be characterized as progressing through four stages: (1) **Isolated Classification** (2015-2019) — treating SLR as a visual pattern recognition problem, where the goal is to map a video clip to a word label, exemplified by early CNN-based approaches and the WLASL benchmark [29]; (2) **Skeleton-Based Recognition** (2018-2022) — leveraging pose estimation (OpenPose, MediaPipe) to extract skeletal keypoints and applying GCN/TCN architectures [6][8][12] for more robust, background-invariant recognition; (3) **Multimodal Fusion** (2022-2024) — combining skeleton, RGB, depth, and optical flow modalities through attention mechanisms and Transformer architectures [7][22][23] to improve accuracy under challenging conditions; and (4) **Semantic Understanding** (2024-present) — the emerging recognition that classification accuracy alone is insufficient, and that practical SLR systems must bridge the gap from gesture labels to communicable language through LLM integration [25][26][27] and scene-contextualized semantic parsing.

The proposed system in this thesis is positioned at the transition from Stage 3 to Stage 4. It inherits the multimodal fusion paradigm (Stage 3: GCN + Visual + Motion Transformer-fused) but extends it with two contributions that push toward Stage 4: (a) the dual-hand combinatorial encoding protocol, which treats gestures as linguistic tokens in a compositional semantic system rather than isolated classification targets, and (b) the LLM enhancement layer, which converts raw token sequences into contextually appropriate natural language through DeepSeek API integration with scene-specific prompts. This positioning — leveraging mature multimodal perception while pioneering LLM-enhanced semantic output — represents the central contribution of this thesis within the broader research trajectory.

# CHAPTER 3: PROPOSED METHOD FOR SIGN LANGUAGE RECOGNITION

This chapter presents the core technical contribution of the thesis: a seven-layer pipeline that converts raw video frames from a commodity RGB camera into natural Chinese sentences. Each layer is described in detail, with specific attention to architectural improvements over baseline approaches and design rationale grounded in the literature reviewed in Chapter 2.

## 3.1 Overall Architecture

The proposed system adopts a seven-layer pipeline architecture, processing video frames sequentially from camera capture to natural language output. Figure 3-1 provides a visual overview of the complete pipeline. The layers are organized as follows:

**Layer 1 — Perception (Section 3.2):** MediaPipe HandLandmarker extracts 21 three-dimensional hand keypoints from each video frame at 128-pixel input resolution. Wrist-relative normalization ensures distance invariance across users. The detector operates on CPU via the XNNPACK delegate, achieving 31.7 FPS.

**Layer 2 — Feature Extraction (Section 3.3):** Three parallel encoders process the keypoint data into complementary feature representations. SpatialGCN (0.15M parameters) encodes hand skeletal topology through graph convolutions with an auxiliary angle encoder that provides explicit geometric priors. LightweightVisualEncoder (0.15M trainable, MobileNetV3-Small backbone frozen at 2.5M) extracts RGB texture features from 96×96 hand ROI crops. MotionEncoder (0.04M) captures inter-frame dynamics through keypoint velocity fields and optical flow histograms with three-path adaptive fusion. The three modality embeddings are integrated through CrossModalFusion (1.9M), a 2-layer Transformer Encoder with learnable modality type embeddings that enable dynamic cross-modal attention weighting.

**Layer 3 — Temporal Modeling (Section 3.4):** The SlowFast TCN (0.7M) processes sequences of fused features through dual temporal pathways. The Slow pathway (kernel 7, dilations [1,2,4], receptive field 43 frames ≈ 1.7 seconds) captures long-range gesture semantics. The Fast pathway (kernel 3, dilations [1,1,2,2]) preserves fine-grained finger dynamics at higher temporal resolution. Lateral convolutions transfer information from Fast to Slow. Temporal mean pooling followed by cross-entropy loss converts frame-level logits to a single sequence-level class prediction, designed for the static gesture recognition task where each recording contains a single held gesture.

**Layer 4 — Rule-Based Recognition (Section 3.5):** A geometric decision tree with clinically validated thresholds provides both a production recognition path and an XAI-compliant fallback. Finger extension is determined by MCP-PIP-DIP joint angles exceeding 165 degrees. The binary finger-state pattern (5 bits, 32 possible combinations) maps to 15 gesture classes. PIP/MCP dual-channel verification applies confidence penalties for inconsistent detections between the two joint measurement channels. Every classification decision traces to specific joint angle measurements and clinical thresholds — a requirement for medical-scenario AI transparency.

**Layer 5 — Semantic Parsing (Section 3.6):** The core innovation of this system — a dual-hand combinatorial encoding protocol. The left hand selects one of seven semantic layers (mode gestures: Good=Number, Seven=Body, Victory=Hospital, Eight=Symptom, Two=Severity, Pinky_Up=Time, One=Phrase). Symmetric dual-hand control gestures (both hands showing the same gesture for 0.5 seconds) provide undo-word (Victory), undo-sentence (Seven), and manual separator (One) functions.

**Layer 6 — LLM Enhancement (Section 3.7):** The DeepSeek Chat API provides two operational modes. In translation mode, raw gesture token sequences are sent with a hospital communication system prompt for word reordering, sentence completion, numerical parsing, and tone customization. A four-level graceful degradation strategy ensures system availability: (1) DeepSeek polished output, (2) semantic chain parser assembled text when API times out, (3) raw token concatenation when no chain matches, (4) rule engine only when DL fails.

**Layer 7 — UI Presentation:** A Vue.js 3.5 single-page application communicates with the FastAPI backend via WebSocket at 25 frames per second. Real-time HTML5 Canvas rendering overlays the 21-keypoint hand skeleton (color-coded: blue left, green right) on the live camera feed. Hand gesture cards display recognition results with confidence percentages. The semantic output panel shows the current token buffer with state-dependent color coding. Operation log and recognition history panels feature auto-scroll. One-click toggles switch between DL/Rule mode and translation/triage mode.

A key architectural principle is the separation between the scene-agnostic and scene-specific layers. Layers 1 through 4 (perception, feature extraction, temporal modeling, rule recognition) are completely independent of the application scenario — they recognize hand gestures regardless of what those gestures mean. Layers 5 and 6 (semantic parsing, LLM enhancement) are scene-configurable through JSON files that define gesture-to-meaning mappings, semantic chain rules, and LLM system prompts. This separation means that migrating the system from hospital triage to banking, airport information, smart home control, or government services requires only replacement of configuration files — the underlying gesture recognition code remains unchanged.

## 3.2 Perception Layer: MediaPipe Hand Keypoint Detection

The perception layer is responsible for converting raw RGB video frames into structured hand keypoint data that downstream layers can process. This conversion must be fast (to meet real-time requirements), accurate (to provide reliable input to subsequent classifiers), and robust (to handle varying lighting, hand orientations, and distances).

### 3.2.1 Input Processing

Each frame received from the camera (original resolution typically 640×480 pixels, JPEG-compressed for WebSocket transmission at 25 FPS) is decoded and downscaled. The longer edge is resized to 128 pixels while maintaining the aspect ratio. This resolution was chosen empirically: 160 pixels (the previous setting) provided marginally higher keypoint accuracy but at ~34ms detection latency, while 128 pixels reduces detection to ~31.5ms with negligible accuracy loss, as the MediaPipe hand landmark model is robust to moderate input downscaling. The trade-off between resolution and speed is particularly important for CPU-only deployment, where every millisecond of detection time directly impacts the system frame rate.

### 3.2.2 MediaPipe HandLandmarker Configuration

The HandLandmarker is configured with the following parameters, chosen based on the clinical validation literature reviewed in Section 2.1:
- **num_hands = 2**: Both hands are detected simultaneously, enabling the dual-hand encoding protocol.
- **min_detection_confidence = 0.4**: Reduced from the default 0.5 to maintain hand tracking persistence during rapid movements and partial occlusions. The literature [2][3] indicates that PIP joint tracking error increases under high-speed motion; lowering the detection threshold prevents premature hand loss at the cost of occasional low-confidence detections, which are filtered by downstream confidence checks.
- **min_tracking_confidence = 0.35**: Further reduced from 0.5 to maintain tracking continuity across frames. The XNNPACK CPU delegate achieves acceptable performance (~31.5ms per detection) for the 25 FPS target.

### 3.2.3 Keypoint Normalization

Raw keypoint coordinates from MediaPipe are in normalized image coordinates (range [0, 1] relative to image width and height). To achieve distance and scale invariance — critical for consistent gesture classification regardless of the user's distance from the camera — first-frame wrist normalization is applied. For each hand, the wrist keypoint (index 0) is used as the reference point, and the palm size is estimated as the Euclidean distance between the wrist (0) and the middle finger MCP joint (9):

palm_scale = || kp_9 - kp_0 ||; kp'_i = (kp_i - kp_0) / palm_scale. This maps the hand to a canonical coordinate frame invariant to camera distance and user position.

### 3.2.4 Handedness Sorting

MediaPipe provides handedness classification (Left/Right) for each detected hand. The raw detection order varies depending on which hand enters the frame first, making it unreliable for the hard-coded left-hand-as-mode convention used throughout the semantic parsing layer. When only one hand is detected, the system uses persistent mode memory — the previously established mode gesture continues to apply for 1.5 seconds after the second hand is lost, providing robustness against brief MediaPipe tracking dropouts.

## 3.3 Feature Extraction Layer: Multi-Modal Encoding

The feature extraction layer transforms the 21 normalized keypoint coordinates and the original video frame into rich, complementary feature representations that capture spatial structure, visual appearance, and temporal dynamics. Three parallel encoders produce modality-specific embeddings, which are then integrated through a Transformer-based fusion module.

### 3.3.1 Spatial Graph Convolutional Network (SpatialGCN)

The SpatialGCN models the hand skeleton as a graph G = (V, E) where the node set V consists of the 21 keypoints and the edge set E consists of 23 bone connections (19 direct bone edges plus 4 functional edges connecting the bases of adjacent fingers, following the hand anatomical topology). Each node i has an initial feature vector h_i^(0) = [x_i, y_i, z_i] representing its normalized 3D coordinates.

The graph convolution operation at layer l aggregates features from each node's neighbors N(i) in the graph:

h_i^(l+1) = ReLU( W^(l) * SUM_{j in N(i)} h_j^(l) / |N(i)| + b^(l) )

Three graph convolution layers are stacked with residual connections, progressively increasing the feature dimension from 3 (input coordinates) through 64 and 128 to 256 (output embedding). A residual connection at each layer adds the input features to the output, enabling gradient flow through deeper graph structures and preventing the over-smoothing problem common in GCNs where node features become indistinguishable after multiple convolution layers.

**Auxiliary Angle Encoder**: A key improvement over standard GCN architectures is the auxiliary angle encoder that computes 10 inter-joint angles directly from the keypoint coordinates. For each finger (thumb through pinky), the MCP-PIP-DIP angle is computed using the law of cosines:

angle = arccos( (a^2 + b^2 - c^2) / (2ab) )

where a, b, c are the Euclidean distances between the three joint points. The 10 angles (2 per finger: MCP-PIP-DIP for the main joint chain, and the inter-finger spread angle between adjacent fingers) are encoded through a small fully-connected network (10→32→64) to produce a 64-dimensional geometric prior vector. This vector is concatenated with the 256-dimensional GCN output and projected through a linear layer to the final 256-dimensional spatial embedding.

The auxiliary angle encoder addresses a limitation of pure GCNs: geometric relationships that are anatomically obvious must otherwise be learned from data. By explicitly encoding joint angles, the model receives strong geometric priors that accelerate learning on limited data [4].

The complete SpatialGCN module (3 GCN layers + angle encoder + projection) contains approximately 0.15M parameters.

### 3.3.2 Lightweight Visual Encoder

While keypoint coordinates capture the geometric configuration of the hand, they discard all photometric information — skin texture, shadows, contours, and the visual context around the hand. The LightweightVisualEncoder complements the geometric features with appearance-based information extracted from the original RGB frame.

The encoder uses a MobileNetV3-Small backbone [31], a state-of-the-art lightweight convolutional network designed through neural architecture search specifically for mobile and edge deployment scenarios. The backbone was pre-trained on ImageNet (1.28 million images, 1000 classes), providing strong general-purpose visual feature extraction capabilities. The classifier head (final fully-connected layer + softmax) is removed, exposing the 576-dimensional feature vector from the global average pooling layer. A trainable two-layer MLP projection head (576→512→512, with ReLU activation and Dropout(0.1) between layers) maps these features to the final 512-dimensional visual embedding.

The backbone's 2.5M parameters are frozen at ImageNet pre-trained values to prevent overfitting on the limited 1,311-sample dataset. The trainable projection head (0.15M) adapts generic visual features to the gesture classification task.

**Input Preparation**: A 96×96 pixel ROI is cropped around the detected hand keypoints. The crop region is computed as the bounding box of all 21 keypoints expanded by a 25-pixel margin in each direction. The crop is resized to exactly 96×96 pixels using bilinear interpolation. Pixel values are normalized from [0, 255] to [0, 1].

**Zero-Input Handling**: Synthetic training samples lack corresponding ROI images (the data generator produces only keypoint sequences). For these samples, the encoder receives a tensor of zeros. A dedicated early-exit path checks `x.abs().sum() == 0` at the start of the forward pass and returns a zero vector immediately, bypassing the MobileNetV3 backbone entirely. This prevents BatchNorm layers in the backbone from producing NaN values due to zero-variance input (a numerical stability issue encountered during development). The model is trained with a mixture of real ROI samples and zero-input synthetic samples, learning to rely more heavily on geometric (GCN) and motion features when visual information is absent.

### 3.3.3 Motion Encoder

The MotionEncoder captures inter-frame dynamics, including subtle within-pose movements (tremors, adjustments) that provide discriminative information beyond static spatial features.

Two input streams are computed from consecutive frame keypoints:

1. **Keypoint Velocity Field (63 dimensions):** For each of the 21 keypoints, the 2D displacement vector (dx, dy) between frames t-1 and t is computed. Additionally, the magnitude of the displacement (sqrt(dx^2 + dy^2)) is included for each keypoint, yielding 21×3 = 63 dimensions. This captures both the direction and speed of each joint's movement.

2. **Optical Flow Histogram (128 dimensions):** The 21 keypoint displacement vectors are aggregated into a flow histogram inspired by optical flow representations in video analysis. The first 21 bins accumulate displacement magnitudes per keypoint, capturing which joints moved most. The remaining 107 bins form a 42-bin directional histogram (0-360 degrees in 8.57-degree increments) accumulated across all keypoints, with each keypoint's contribution weighted by its displacement magnitude. This captures the dominant motion direction and distribution.

The two streams are processed through separate small fully-connected networks (63→64→64 and 128→64→64), then fused through a learned softmax-weighted combination:

alpha = softmax(W_alpha * concat(v1, v2))
fused = alpha_1 * v1_out + alpha_2 * v2_out

The fused 64-dimensional representation is projected to the final 128-dimensional motion embedding. Total parameters: approximately 0.04M.

### 3.3.4 CrossModalFusion: Transformer-Based Modality Integration

The three modality embeddings — visual (512-dim), spatial (256-dim), and motion (128-dim) — represent fundamentally different types of information. Simple concatenation treats them as interchangeable feature channels, missing the opportunity to model cross-modal interactions. The CrossModalFusion module uses a Transformer Encoder to enable each modality to attend to information from the other two modalities.

**Architecture**: Each modality embedding is first projected to a common dimensionality of d_model = 256 through a dedicated linear layer:

V_proj = W_v * V + b_v   (512→256)
S_proj = W_s * S + b_s   (256→256)
M_proj = W_m * M + b_m   (128→256)

The three projected tokens are augmented with **learnable modality type embeddings** — three trainable 256-dimensional vectors E_v, E_s, E_m that encode the source modality of each token. These are added to the projected tokens before entering the Transformer:

V_token = V_proj + E_v
S_token = S_proj + E_s
M_token = M_proj + E_m

The three tokens [V_token, S_token, M_token] are concatenated into a sequence of length 3 and processed through a 2-layer Transformer Encoder with h = 8 attention heads, feed-forward dimension d_ff = 1024, and dropout rate 0.1. After cross-modal self-attention, global mean pooling across the 3 token positions produces a unified 256-dimensional fused feature vector.

Learnable modality embeddings enable attention heads to dynamically weight modalities — favoring geometric features under poor lighting where visual features are degraded.

The complete CrossModalFusion module contains approximately 1.9M parameters, the largest single component in the DL pipeline. The parameter distribution reflects the importance of learning cross-modal interactions: the fusion module is allocated nearly twice the parameters of the TCN (0.7M) because it must model pairwise relationships between three heterogeneous feature spaces.

## 3.4 Temporal Modeling Layer: SlowFast TCN with CE Pooling

The temporal modeling layer processes a sequence of T fused feature vectors (one per video frame, each 256-dimensional) and produces a single gesture class prediction for the entire sequence. This is a sequence-to-label mapping: T input frames → 1 output class.

### 3.4.1 Dual-Pathway Design

The SlowFast TCN architecture (illustrated in Figure 2-3, Chapter 2) employs two parallel pathways operating at different temporal resolutions:

**Slow Pathway** (kernel size 7, dilations [1, 2, 4], channel progression [256→64→128→128]):
- Purpose: Capture long-range semantic context spanning complete gesture phrases.
- Receptive field: RF = 1 + (7-1)×(1+2+4) = 43 frames ≈ 1.7 seconds at 25 FPS.
- The 1.7-second window covers: hand entry into the gesture pose (0.2s), stable hold (1.0-1.5s), and gesture release/transition (0.2-0.5s) — the complete lifecycle of a single gesture.
- Large kernel (7) and increasing dilations provide exponentially growing temporal context without increasing parameter count.

**Fast Pathway** (kernel size 3, dilations [1, 1, 2, 2], channel progression [256→32→32→64→64]):
- Purpose: Preserve fine-grained temporal dynamics at high temporal resolution.
- Smaller kernel (3) retains sensitivity to rapid finger movements and brief pose adjustments.
- Lower channel dimensions keep the parameter count minimal — the Fast pathway's job is to capture high-frequency temporal detail, not to model complex semantic relationships (which is the Slow pathway's role).

**Lateral Connections**: After each stage in the Fast pathway (after dilations 1 and 2), a 1×1 convolution projects Fast features to the Slow pathway's channel dimension and adds them to the corresponding Slow stage. These lateral connections enable the Slow pathway to benefit from the Fast pathway's fine-grained temporal information without increasing its own temporal resolution, maintaining the computational efficiency of the two-pathway design.

After both pathways complete their processing, the Slow and Fast output features are concatenated and projected through a final output layer that produces per-frame class logits: [B, T, num_classes].

### 3.4.2 Loss Function Design: CE + Temporal Pooling vs CTC

The choice of loss function for temporal modeling is fundamentally determined by the nature of the recognition task. This system performs **static gesture recognition**: the user holds a single hand pose for the duration of each 2-second recording, and the expected output is a single class label. This is a **sequence-to-label** (many-to-one) mapping.

The standard approach for sequence-to-label classification with neural networks is temporal pooling followed by cross-entropy loss:

logits = TCN(features)                    # [B, T, C] — per-frame class logits
pooled = mean(logits, dim=1)              # [B, C] — average over time dimension
loss = CrossEntropy(pooled, labels)       # scalar

The temporal mean pooling operation converts T per-frame predictions into a single sequence-level prediction by averaging across the time dimension. Each frame contributes equally to the final prediction, which is appropriate for static gestures where all frames contain the same gesture (the averaging suppresses frame-to-frame noise in the logit predictions).

An alternative approach would be Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) [32], widely used in speech recognition and continuous sign language recognition. CTC introduces a blank token and learns an alignment between input frames and output labels, handling variable-length output sequences. non-blank outputs
- The additional hyperparameters (blank index, CTC beam width) provide no benefit for single-label prediction

The CE with temporal pooling approach, by contrast, directly supervises the sequence-level prediction and converges more stably on limited data. The comparison is illustrated in Figure 2-5 (Chapter 2).

### 3.4.3 Design Rationale: Why TCN Rather Than Transformer

The dominant architecture for sequence modeling in contemporary deep learning is the Transformer, and a natural question is why this system uses a TCN instead. Four considerations drove this choice: (1) Parameter efficiency — 0.7M vs. 10M+ for Transformer, critical on 1,311 samples; (2) Causal convolutions naturally prevent future-frame leakage in real-time inference; (3) Pure convolutional architecture exports cleanly to ONNX; (4) Receptive field precisely engineerable (43 frames for 1.7s gesture phrases). GCAR [13] validated TCN efficiency at 0.69M on WLASL; MSE-GCN [17] and AM-GCN [8] further support this design choice.

## 3.5 Rule-Based Recognition Layer: XAI Decision Tree

The rule-based layer provides instantaneous, explainable classifications using a geometric decision tree adapted from clinical goniometric protocols [1][4].

### 3.5.1 Algorithm Description

The recognition algorithm proceeds through four stages:

**Stage 1:** Finger extension angle theta_f = angle(kp_MCP, kp_PIP, kp_DIP); extended if >165deg (Hamaguchi [4], r=0.78).
**Stage 2:** 5-bit pattern (32 combinations) mapped to 15 gesture classes. Two vs. Victory disambiguated by index-middle fingertip Euclidean distance (threshold 0.06 normalized units).
**Stage 3:** Dual-channel PIP/MCP verification — PIP joints exhibit the highest MediaPipe error (20-30deg [2][3]). Per-finger disagreement applies 12% confidence penalty.
**Stage 4:** 5-bit pattern maps to gesture name. PIP-sensitive classes (Victory, Six, Three, Four, Seven, Nine [3]) receive additional scrutiny.

### 3.5.2 XAI Compliance

A distinguishing feature of the rule engine is its compliance with Explainable AI (XAI) principles. Every classification decision can be traced to specific, measurable physical quantities:

- "Gesture classified as Closed_Fist because: MCP-PIP-DIP angles for all five fingers were measured below 165 degrees (thumb: 142°, index: 138°, middle: 145°, ring: 151°, pinky: 147°)"
- "Gesture classified as Victory because: Index MCP-PIP-DIP angle = 172° (>165°, extended), Middle MCP-PIP-DIP angle = 168° (>165°, extended), other three fingers all <165° (flexed), index-middle tip distance = 0.12 normalized units (>0.06, separated)"

This traceability is essential for the medical triage application scenario. In a healthcare context, an AI system that cannot explain its decisions faces regulatory and trust barriers. The rule engine's XAI compliance ensures that when the system outputs a classification, the clinical staff can verify the reasoning by examining the measured joint angles.

## 3.6 Semantic Parsing Layer: Dual-Hand Combinatorial Encoding

The semantic parsing layer represents the core conceptual innovation of this thesis: a protocol that maps a compact vocabulary of 15 gestures to a rich semantic space of 70 slots through combinatorial encoding. This section describes the encoding scheme, the SentenceRecorder state machine that manages the recording lifecycle, and the design rationale behind key protocol choices.

### 3.6.1 Design Philosophy

Traditional sign language recognition maps gestures to meanings in a one-to-one fashion: each gesture corresponds to exactly one word or concept. Real human sign languages achieve far greater efficiency through grammatical composition — handshape, location, movement, and facial expression combine to create meaning beyond the sum of individual components.

The dual-hand combinatorial protocol applies this compositional principle in a simplified, learnable form. The non-dominant (left) hand selects a semantic layer; the dominant (right) hand selects a specific meaning within that layer. With 7 mode gestures and 10 content gestures, the system achieves 70 semantic slots from only 15 base gestures — a 4.7-fold encoding efficiency over one-to-one mapping. This design mirrors the linguistic distinction between function words (grammatical context) and content words (specific meaning), applied to the gestural domain.

### 3.6.2 Gesture Vocabulary

The 15-gesture vocabulary was designed to satisfy three criteria: (1) gestures must be reliably distinguishable by both the rule engine and the DL model, (2) the finger configurations must be physically comfortable for extended use, and (3) the gesture-to-meaning mapping must be intuitive and learnable.

**Seven Mode Gestures (Left Hand):**

| Gesture | Finger Pattern | Semantic Layer | Rationale |

| Good | Thumb up | Number (0-9) | Natural association: thumbs-up = counting |
| Seven | Thumb+Index+Middle | Body Parts | Three extended fingers = body mapping |
| Victory | Index+Middle spread | Hospital Ops | V-sign = medical context |
| Eight | Thumb+Index | Symptoms | L-shape = symptom checklist |
| Two | Index+Middle together | Severity | Two fingers = degree/level |
| Pinky_Up | Pinky only | Time/Frequency | Pinky promise = time commitment |

**Ten Content Gestures (Right Hand):**

The content vocabulary uses the complete set of distinguishable finger-count patterns: Closed_Fist (0 extended), One (1), Two (2), Three (3), Four (4), Open_Palm (5), Six (thumb+pinky), Seven (thumb+index+middle), Eight (thumb+index), Nine (ring flexed, 4 extended). Four gestures — One, Two, Seven, Eight — appear in both mode and content sets, with their role determined by which hand shows them (left=mode, right=content).

### 3.6.3 SentenceRecorder State Machine

The SentenceRecorder finite state machine (FSM), illustrated in Figure 3-5, governs the recording lifecycle through three states: IDLE (awaiting start trigger), RECORDING (accumulating gesture tokens with hold-duration and cooldown enforcement), and OUTPUT (generating the final sentence with automatic reset). The FSM design follows three principles: (1) dual-pathway initiation — both a discrete transition (Closed_Fist to Open_Palm, the natural "preparing to speak" motion) and a sustained-hold fallback (both Open_Palm for 1.0s) trigger recording start; (2) bounded accumulation — gesture tokens are accepted under a minimum hold duration and inter-gesture cooldown to filter transient noise, with an idle timeout preventing indefinite hanging; and (3) symmetric control — dual-hand patterns where both hands show the same mode gesture serve control functions (undo-word, undo-sentence, manual separator) without conflicting with normal mode+content input. The complete timing constants, transition conditions, and engineering implementation are detailed in Section 4.3.1.

**Mode Persistence:** When MediaPipe briefly loses tracking on one hand (a common occurrence during rapid transitions or partial occlusions), the system does not immediately forget the current mode layer. The persistent mode memory retains the last established mode for 1.5 seconds, during which single-hand content gestures are interpreted within the persisted mode context. This provides robustness against brief tracking dropouts while preventing stale mode information from persisting indefinitely.

### 3.6.4 Semantic Chain Parser

The accumulated token sequence (e.g., ["疼", "，", "头", "，", "严重"]) is not yet a natural sentence. The TemporalSemanticParser matches the token sequence against a database of 40 general-purpose semantic chains and 65 hospital-specific medical chains using longest-suffix matching. The parser greedily matches the longest chain that is a suffix of the current token sequence, allowing prefix tokens to be interpreted later as more tokens arrive.

For example, the sequence ["疼", "头", "严重"] matches the hospital chain ["疼", "头"] with category "symptom_description", while the trailing "严重" will be matched when more context arrives or when the recording ends. The matched chain provides a template for assembling the raw tokens into a base sentence, which is then sent to the DeepSeek API for final polishing.

## 3.7 LLM Enhancement Layer

The LLM enhancement layer transforms raw, potentially fragmented semantic token sequences into natural, contextually appropriate Chinese sentences. It provides two operational modes that share the same underlying API infrastructure.

### 3.7.1 Translation Mode

In translation mode, the accumulated gesture token sequence is sent to the DeepSeek Chat API (model: deepseek-chat) with a hospital communication system prompt. The prompt instructs the model to perform: (1) word reordering — arranging tokens from conceptual order (head→symptom→severity) to natural Chinese word order; (2) sentence completion — adding grammatical particles, aspect markers, and cohesive devices that are absent from the gesture token stream; (3) numerical parsing — interpreting number sequences in context (e.g., "3" + "天" → "三天"); (4) tone customization — adapting the register to the medical context (polite, clear, informative); and (5) dialogue coherence — maintaining consistency with the sentence history from previous recordings.

The API call is made synchronously within the recording end sequence, with a connection timeout of 1.5 seconds and a read timeout of 2.0 seconds. If the API does not respond within this window (due to network latency or service unavailability), the system gracefully degrades to the semantic chain parser's base sentence output. This design ensures that the core recognition functionality is never blocked by external API dependency.

### 3.7.2 Triage Mode

Triage mode extends the translation functionality with interactive multi-turn medical consultation, inspired by the clinical dialogue capabilities of LLMs demonstrated in [26] and [27]. When the user switches to triage mode:

1. **Symptom Description:** The patient describes their primary complaint through gestures. The symptom layer (mode=Eight) + body layer (mode=Seven) + severity layer (mode=Two) enable detailed symptom communication (e.g., "头" + "疼" + "严重" = "severe headache").

2. **LLM Follow-up:** DeepSeek processes the symptom description and generates a closed-ended follow-up question targeting the most diagnostically informative missing information. The question is designed for yes/no answers to accommodate the limited gesture vocabulary.

3. **Patient Response:** The patient answers using the Common Phrases layer (mode=One): Six = "是" (Yes), Nine = "不是" (No).

4. **Iteration:** Steps 2-3 repeat for 2-3 rounds, with each round targeting a different diagnostic dimension (onset timing, associated symptoms, aggravating/relieving factors).

5. **Department Recommendation:** After confirming that no additional symptoms exist, DeepSeek recommends the most appropriate medical department based on the collected symptom profile.

The triage conversation runs asynchronously using Python's asyncio run_in_executor, preventing the API call from blocking the frame processing pipeline. The conversation history is displayed in the UI in real time as messages are exchanged.

### 3.7.3 Graceful Degradation

The system implements a four-level graceful degradation strategy to ensure availability under all conditions:

1. **Level 1 (Full Service):** DeepSeek API available → polished natural language output with contextual adaptation and dialogue coherence.
2. **Level 2 (API Timeout):** Semantic chain parser assembles base sentence from matched chains → grammatically simpler but still meaningful output.
3. **Level 3 (No Chain Match):** Raw token concatenation with comma separators → fragmented but informative (shows exactly what gestures were recognized).
4. **Level 4 (DL/API Failure):** Rule engine only → geometric gesture classification still functions, system remains operational for basic communication.


## 3.8 Chapter Summary

The proposed method spans seven processing layers, each contributing a distinct function to the gesture-to-language pipeline. The perception layer provides robust, real-time hand keypoint detection with clinical-grade normalization. The rule engine provides XAI-compliant, instantaneous classification with clinical traceability, while the dual-hand combinatorial encoding protocol achieves 4.7-fold semantic efficiency through compositional mode+content separation.

# CHAPTER 5: SYSTEM DESIGN AND DEVELOPMENT

This chapter addresses the engineering dimension of the project: translating the algorithmic contributions described in Chapter 3 into a complete, deployable software system. Requirements analysis, system context, functional design, and user interface implementation are covered systematically.

## 5.1 Requirement Analysis

Requirements were derived from the task book, hospital triage deployment considerations, and real-time systems best practices.

### 5.1.1 Functional Requirements

The system shall satisfy the following functional requirements:

**FR1:** Real-time dual-hand detection via standard RGB webcam. **FR2:** 15-gesture recognition (7 mode + 10 content, 4 dual-role). **FR3:** Dual-mode recognition with one-click rule/DL switching. **FR4:** Dual-hand combinatorial encoding (70 semantic slots). **FR5:** Natural Chinese sentence output via chain matching + DeepSeek polishing. **FR6:** AI triage with multi-turn follow-up and department recommendation. **FR7:** Web GUI with real-time skeleton, gesture cards, FPS monitoring. **FR8:** Video upload for offline testing. **FR9:** Scene migration via JSON configuration replacement.

### 5.1.2 Non-Functional Requirements

**NFR1:** Detection >=25 FPS (achieved 31.7 FPS). **NFR2:** DL parameters <10M (3.4M, 66% margin). **NFR3:** CPU-only operation. **NFR4:** End-to-end latency <500ms. **NFR5:** Four-level graceful degradation. **NFR6:** Cross-platform browser accessibility.

## 5.2 System Context

**Scene Portability Mechanism:** The separation between scene-agnostic and scene-specific layers (described in Section 3.1) is the key enabler of system portability. The gesture recognition pipeline (perception, feature extraction, temporal modeling, rule recognition) is entirely independent of the application scenario. The semantic layer (mappings, chains, prompts) is defined through three replaceable JSON configuration files:
- `config/semantic_chains.json` — 40 general-purpose semantic chains (emergency, emotion, greeting, daily expression categories)
- `config/hospital_chains.json` — 65 hospital-specific medical semantic chains (symptom description, procedure requests, department navigation)
- DeepSeek system prompt — embedded in deepseek_client.py, configurable per scenario

## 5.3 System Features

The system provides four core features accessible through the web interface.

### 5.3.1 Feature 1: Sign Language Translation Mode

The workflow: (1) WebSocket at 25 FPS; (2) start via both-hands transition or 1.0s hold; (3) accumulate with 0.3s/0.35s/15s timing and auto-separator; (4) end via 1.5s hold or timeout; (5) chain matching + DeepSeek polishing with graceful fallback.

**Error Handling:** MediaPipe hand loss triggers persistent mode memory (1.5s window) preventing single-frame dropout from breaking mode+content combinations. DeepSeek API unavailability falls back to chain-matched output. Complete API and DL failure falls back to rule-engine-only operation (Level 4).

### 5.3.2 Feature 2: AI Triage Consultation Mode

**Operational Flow:**
1. User switches from translation mode to triage mode via the mode toggle button (UI updates to show "Triage Mode" with distinct color).
2. The recommendation appears in a highlighted panel with the department name and brief rationale.

### 5.3.3 Feature 3: DL/Rule Dual-Mode Switching

**Operational Flow:**
1. System defaults to rule mode on startup (instantaneous, XAI-compliant, zero warm-up).
2. DL buffers are cleared, and the daemon thread enters idle state (still running but skipping processing).

### 5.3.4 Feature 4: Scene Configuration Migration

**Operational Flow:**
1. System ships with hospital triage as the default scene configuration.
2. To migrate to a new scene, the deployer replaces three JSON files: semantic_chains.json (general chains), hospital_chains.json (scene-specific chains, renamed to reflect the new scene), and updates the DeepSeek system prompt in deepseek_client.py.
3. No code modification is required. The gesture recognition pipeline (layers 1-4) remains identical. The SentenceRecorder state machine, semantic parser, and LLM client read their configuration from the JSON files on startup.
4. The mode layer gesture-to-meaning mappings (MODE_MAPS in sentence_recorder.py) are also scene-configurable — each scene can define its own semantic layers and content slot meanings.

## 5.4 System Interface

The web-based user interface, built with Vue.js 3.5 as a single-page application, communicates with the FastAPI backend through a single WebSocket connection, eliminating multi-channel synchronization complexity. The design follows three interaction paradigms that collectively shape the information architecture.

**Paradigm 1 — Spatial Consistency:** The interface is organized as a two-row layout with fixed camera panel (640x480px, left) and scrollable information sidebar (right). This spatial partitioning reflects the natural visual flow: primary input (camera feed with real-time HTML5 Canvas skeleton overlay, blue for left hand, green for right) occupies the dominant left position, while interpreted output (gesture cards, semantic display, operation log, confidence bar) flows vertically on the right. The camera panel displays the application branding image when inactive, maintaining visual continuity. A horizontal button bar below the camera provides all controls: camera toggle, DL/Rule switch, translate/triage mode, hand swap, video upload, and force-end recording.

**Paradigm 2 — State-Visible Feedback:** Every system state transition produces an immediate, visually distinct UI change. The recording lifecycle (IDLE to RECORDING to OUTPUT) is surfaced through color-coded semantic output: blue for IDLE (instructional idle message), orange for active recording (real-time token buffer display). Gesture recognition confidence is dual-encoded as both a numerical percentage and a color-graded progress bar (red below 30%, orange 30-60%, green above 60%). WebSocket connectivity is monitored with a real-time indicator. The DL/Rule mode toggle changes button color to reflect the active recognition path. This principle ensures the user always perceives system state and confidence without conscious effort.

**Paradigm 3 — Chronological Transparency:** All user actions and system responses are logged in temporal order with automatic scroll-to-latest. The operation log records lifecycle events (start recording, separator inserted, word undone, sentence output) with type-specific color coding (blue/gray/red/green). The recognition history table stores completed sentences with timestamps, raw gesture tokens, and DeepSeek-polished output, enabling post-hoc communication review. New entries append to the bottom following natural chronological reading direction. In triage mode, an additional panel shares the bottom row in a 50/50 split, displaying the multi-turn AI consultation: patient messages derived from gesture input (gray, right-aligned), AI follow-up questions (blue, left-aligned), and department recommendations (green-highlighted).

# CHAPTER 6: CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

## 6.1 Conclusion

This thesis presented the design, implementation, and experimental evaluation of a lightweight multimodal real-time sign language recognition system for barrier-free deaf communication. The system addresses an accessibility need — enabling barrier-free communication for deaf individuals in practical daily scenarios — through a carefully engineered pipeline that balances accuracy, efficiency, explainability, and deployability.

The system's core technical contribution is a seven-layer pipeline architecture that converts raw video frames from a commodity RGB camera into natural Chinese sentences. The perception layer achieves 31.7 FPS hand keypoint detection on CPU, exceeding the 25 FPS task book requirement. The LLM enhancement layer integrates DeepSeek API for semantic reassembly and multi-turn triage consultation, with a four-level graceful degradation strategy ensuring system availability under all conditions.

Experimental evaluation on a dataset of 1,311 samples (351 real with ROI capture + 960 synthetic with systematic domain randomization) demonstrated: (1) the multimodal DL model achieving 82.8% test accuracy, representing a 51.7 percentage point improvement over the rule engine's 31.1% on real user data; (2) ablation experiments quantifying modality contributions — GCN spatial topology as foundational (76.6pp contribution), motion dynamics as substantial (31.6pp), and visual texture as complementary (9.6pp); (3) stable training convergence with 90.6% peak validation accuracy at epoch 37 under cosine annealing; and (4) production performance of 31.7 FPS detection speed and 32.1ms processing latency, stable across 92 recording sessions.

The system should be understood as a proof-of-concept validated through algorithmic metrics and self-collected data. The dataset of 1,311 samples is modest by deep learning standards, no formal usability testing with deaf participants has been conducted, and the reported 82.8% test accuracy—while representing a 51.7 percentage point improvement over the rule engine baseline on the same test set—should be interpreted within the context of the specific user, lighting conditions, and gesture vocabulary used in the experiments. These limitations are explicitly addressed in the future work directions below.

Before enumerating the innovations, it is important to honestly acknowledge the limitations of the current work. The dataset of 1,311 samples, while systematically collected with domain randomization, is modest in scale and derived from a single participant — generalization to diverse users with different hand anatomies, signing speeds, and environmental conditions remains to be validated. These limitations are not fundamental flaws but represent the natural boundary between academic prototyping and real-world deployment — they define the scope within which the following contributions should be understood.

The five key innovations of this work are:

1. **Dual-Hand Combinatorial Encoding Protocol:** A novel interaction paradigm achieving compositional semantics (70 slots from 15 gestures) through the separation of mode (semantic layer selection) and content (meaning specification) across the two hands. The protocol achieves 4.7-fold encoding efficiency over one-to-one gesture-to-meaning mapping, with auto-separator insertion on mode transitions eliminating the need for explicit delimiter gestures.

2. **Rule/DL Dual-Mode Complementary Architecture:** The rule engine provides population-level, XAI-compliant, instantaneous (<1ms) recognition with full clinical traceability, while the DL model provides data-driven, user-adaptive recognition (82.8%) that generalizes to individual anatomical variation. Both modes share a common downstream semantic pipeline, and their complementary error patterns — rule excels where DL struggles (Closed_Fist 94.1%, Pinky_Up 100%) and vice versa (Eight 100%, Good 100%, Nine 100%) — motivate their coexistence.

3. **Multimodal Lightweight Feature Fusion with Quantitative Validation:** Three encoders spanning spatial (GCN), temporal (motion), and appearance (visual) modalities are fused through a Transformer with learnable modality embeddings. At 3.4M total trainable parameters — 66% below the 10M task budget — the model runs on CPU via ONNX Runtime. Systematic ablation experiments provide quantitative evidence for each modality's unique contribution, validating the architectural investment in multimodal fusion.

4. **Scene-Transferable Architecture:** The gesture recognition pipeline (layers 1-4, representing the majority of the engineering effort) is completely scene-agnostic. Semantic mappings, chain rules, and LLM prompts are JSON-configurable, enabling migration from hospital triage to banking, airport, smart home, or government services through configuration replacement alone — no code modification required.

5. **Complete Gesture-to-Language Pipeline with LLM Integration:** Beyond isolated gesture classification, the system achieves end-to-end conversion from raw hand movements through semantic parsing to natural language sentences, with an LLM-enhanced multi-turn triage consultation capability that represents a unique integration among comparable sign language recognition systems.

## 6.2 Future Work

Seven directions for future improvement are identified, ordered from immediate practical enhancements to longer-term research directions:

1. **User Testing:** Formal SUS studies with deaf participants to validate gesture vocabulary and identify ergonomic improvements.
2. **Mobile Deployment:** Smartphone packaging of the complete CPU pipeline.
